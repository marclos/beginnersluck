The Time Value Problem

Key Question: How do we compare costs today with benefits decades or centuries from now?

  • Clean energy infrastructure: costs now, benefits for 50+ years
  • Climate mitigation: costs now, benefits for centuries
  • Toxic waste cleanup: costs now, prevents harm for generations

Why Discounting Matters

Environmental policies involve intertemporal trade-offs:

  • Initial investment costs (present)
  • Operating and maintenance costs (ongoing)
  • Environmental benefits (long-term)
  • Health improvements (delayed)

The discount rate determines how we value the future

Future Value: The Basics

Starting point: How does money grow over time?

\[FV = PV \times (1 + r)^t\]

Where:

  • \(FV\) = Future Value (what you’ll have)
  • \(PV\) = Present Value (what you start with)
  • \(r\) = interest rate (as decimal, e.g., 0.05 = 5%)
  • \(t\) = time periods (usually years)

Future Value: Step-by-Step

Example: $100 invested at 5% annual rate

Year 1: \[FV_1 = 100 \times (1 + 0.05)^1 = 100 \times 1.05 = \$105\]

Year 2: \[FV_2 = 100 \times (1 + 0.05)^2 = 100 \times 1.1025 = \$110.25\]

Year 3: \[FV_3 = 100 \times (1 + 0.05)^3 = 100 \times 1.157625 = \$115.76\]

Notice: Each year compounds on the previous total

Future Value Over 25 Years

After 25 years: $338.64 (more than 3× the original investment)

Present Value: The Reverse Problem

Question: What is $100 of future benefit worth today?

Rearrange the formula: \[PV = \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^t}\]

This is discounting: Converting future values to present-day equivalents

Present Value: Step-by-Step

Question: What is $100 received in 3 years worth today at 5% discount rate?

Step 1: Calculate the discount factor \[(1 + 0.05)^3 = 1.157625\]

Step 2: Divide future value by discount factor \[PV = \frac{100}{1.157625} = \$86.38\]

Interpretation: $86.38 today is equivalent to $100 in 3 years (at 5% rate)

Present Value Example: 25 Years

How much is $100 of benefit in 25 years worth today?

At 5% discount rate: \[PV = \frac{100}{(1 + 0.05)^{25}} = \frac{100}{3.386} = \$29.53\]

At 1% discount rate: \[PV = \frac{100}{(1 + 0.01)^{25}} = \frac{100}{1.282} = \$78.00\]

The choice of discount rate dramatically affects present values!

The Discount Rate’s Impact

Extreme Case: 100 Years

Present value of $100 benefit in 100 years:

At 1% discount rate: \[PV = \frac{100}{(1.01)^{100}} = \$37.00\]

At 5% discount rate: \[PV = \frac{100}{(1.05)^{100}} = \$0.76\]

At 5%, benefits a century from now are essentially worthless today!

Long-term Environmental Damages

The Climate Change Challenge

Why discount rates matter for climate policy:

  1. Long time horizons: GHG emissions cause damages centuries into future
  2. Irreversibility: Many climate impacts cannot be undone
  3. Intergenerational equity: High discount rates devalue future generations
  4. Catastrophic risks: Possibility of severe, irreversible damages

The Stern Review (2006): Used ~1.4% rate → aggressive action warranted

Traditional economics: Often uses 3-7% → less urgent action

Components of the Discount Rate

Social Discount Rate = Pure Time Preference + Growth Effect

\[r = \rho + \eta \times g\]

Where:

  • \(\rho\) = pure time preference (impatience)
  • \(\eta\) = elasticity of marginal utility (inequality aversion)
  • \(g\) = expected consumption growth rate

Each component is ethically debatable

Pure Time Preference (ρ)

Question: Should we value people in the future less than people today?

Ethical arguments:

  • \(\rho = 0\): Future people matter equally (Stern Review approach)
  • \(\rho > 0\): We naturally prefer present over future (Nordhaus approach)

Example impact: - If \(\rho = 0\) and other components sum to 1.4%, total rate = 1.4% - If \(\rho = 1.5\%\) and other components sum to 1.4%, total rate = 2.9%

Growth Effect (η × g)

Question: Will future generations be richer than us?

Logic: If future is richer, they can better afford costs, so we discount less

Consumption growth (g): - Optimistic: \(g = 2\%\) per year - Pessimistic: \(g = 1\%\) per year

Inequality aversion (η): - High \(\eta = 2\): Strong preference for helping poorer people - Low \(\eta = 1\): Weak preference for redistribution

Example Calculation

Calculate social discount rate:

Assumptions: - Pure time preference: \(\rho = 0.5\%\) - Inequality aversion: \(\eta = 1.5\) - Growth rate: \(g = 2\%\)

\[r = 0.005 + (1.5 \times 0.02) = 0.005 + 0.03 = 0.035\]

Result: 3.5% social discount rate

Real-World Examples

U.S. Government (OMB Circular A-4): - Base case: 3% and 7% - Lower rates for intergenerational projects

UK Green Book: - 3.5% declining to 3% after 30 years - Further declines for longer horizons

France: - 4% for near-term - Declining scale for climate projects

Policy Implications

Ethical Considerations

The discounting debate raises fundamental questions:

  1. Intergenerational justice: Do we owe duties to future generations?
  2. Uncertainty: What if damages are worse than expected?
  3. Irreversibility: Should we discount irreplaceable natural capital?
  4. Catastrophic risk: How to value low-probability, high-impact events?

No purely technical answer—requires ethical and political judgment

Alternative Approaches

Beyond traditional discounting:

  1. Declining discount rates: Lower rates for distant future (UK approach)
  2. Dual discounting: Different rates for different types of impacts
  3. Precautionary principle: Conservative approach under uncertainty
  4. Safe minimum standards: Protect critical thresholds regardless of NPV
  5. Sustainability constraints: Maintain natural capital stocks

Summary: Key Takeaways

  1. Discount rates convert future values to present equivalents

    • Higher rates → future matters less
    • Lower rates → future matters more

  2. Formula: \(PV = \frac{FV}{(1 + r)^t}\)

  3. For long-term environmental problems:

    • Rate choice is ethically loaded
    • Small rate differences = huge outcome differences
    • Climate change most sensitive to this choice

  4. Policy challenge: Balance economic efficiency with intergenerational equity

Discussion Questions

  1. Is it ethical to use positive pure time preference rates for climate policy?

  2. If future generations are expected to be wealthier, should we do less for them today?

  3. How should we account for catastrophic but uncertain climate risks?

  4. Should discount rates differ for different types of environmental damages?

  5. What role should economic efficiency play versus ethical duties to the future?
